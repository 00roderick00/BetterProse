# PATTERNS

The BetterProse ruleset. Part I holds the general prose rules, Part II the voice rules for Roderick Jones's writing, Part III the precedence rules that say what happens when they collide. Every rule in Part I is defensible to an editor who has never heard of a language model: each one names a way a sentence fails at its job.

---

## Part I — Prose quality (BP-01 to BP-16)

Organised by what each rule protects.

### Precision

**BP-01. Vague attribution.** A claim attributed to "experts," "observers," or "analysts" is a claim with no owner; name the source or cut the claim.

> *Before:* Experts warn that undersea cables are increasingly vulnerable to sabotage.
> *After:* A 2023 NATO assessment names undersea cables among the alliance's least defended assets.

**BP-02. Weasel words.** "Some argue," "it could be said," "many believe" let the writer float an opinion without holding it; commit to the claim, attribute it, or drop it.

> *Before:* Some would argue that the sanctions have failed.
> *After:* The sanctions have failed: exports returned to pre-war levels within a year.

**BP-03. False specificity.** Language that wears the costume of precision without its content ("numerous studies," "up to tenfold," "in recent years") should be replaced with a real figure or an honest generality.

> *Before:* Numerous studies show container traffic has grown by up to 300 per cent in recent years.
> *After:* Container traffic through the port has tripled since 2015.

**BP-04. Synonym cycling.** Calling one thing by three names ("the vessel... the ship... the craft") to avoid repetition makes the reader do bookkeeping; keep one name per thing, and cut echoes only when the repetition does no work.

> *Before:* The tanker anchored offshore. The vessel refused inspection. The ship then went dark.
> *After:* The tanker anchored offshore, refused inspection, and then went dark.

### Economy

**BP-05. Filler.** Phrases that add syllables but no meaning ("in order to," "it is important to note that," "at this point in time") get cut on sight.

> *Before:* It is important to note that in order to reach the terminal, convoys must cross the bridge.
> *After:* Convoys must cross the bridge to reach the terminal.

**BP-06. Throat-clearing.** Sentences that announce what the writing is about to do ("Let us now turn to," "It is worth examining") delay the point; do the thing instead of announcing it.

> *Before:* It is worth examining how the insurers responded to the seizure.
> *After:* The insurers responded to the seizure within a week: premiums for the strait doubled.

**BP-07. Generic conclusions.** An ending that could close any piece on any subject ("the coming years will be decisive," "only time will tell") closes none of them; end on the piece's own material.

> *Before:* Whatever happens next, one thing is certain: challenging times lie ahead for the region.
> *After:* The convoy system now protects forty ships a week. Nobody has yet said who pays for it in year two.

**BP-08. Restating headings.** A one-line paragraph that repeats the heading above it ("**Deterrence.** Deterrence matters.") is padding; start with the first real sentence.

> *Before:* **The insurance market.** Insurance plays a central role here. When Lloyd's reclassified the Red Sea...
> *After:* **The insurance market.** When Lloyd's reclassified the Red Sea...

**BP-09. Outline-shaped prose.** Writing organised as a template rather than an argument, the formulaic "challenges and future prospects" section, the list of bolded labels each followed by one thin sentence, should be dissolved back into prose that argues.

> *Before:* **Challenges:** The port faces congestion. **Opportunities:** Trade may grow. **Outlook:** The future is uncertain.
> *After:* The port's congestion is the constraint on any growth in trade, and no dredging is funded before 2027.

### Honesty

**BP-10. Inflated significance.** Declaring that an event "marks a pivotal moment" or "represents a turning point" asserts importance instead of demonstrating it; state what happened and let the facts carry the weight.

> *Before:* The blockade marked a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional trade.
> *After:* The blockade forced every carrier but two to reroute around the Cape.

**BP-11. Promotional register.** Words that sell rather than describe ("vibrant," "world-class," "nestled," "boasts") belong in brochures; description earns belief, promotion spends it.

> *Before:* The city boasts a vibrant port district and a world-class exchange.
> *After:* The port district handles a fifth of the country's imports; the exchange clears most of its currency trades.

**BP-12. Fake depth.** A participial clause tacked onto a sentence to gesture at significance ("...highlighting the interconnected nature of global trade") adds the sound of analysis without any; either make the buried claim a real sentence with its own evidence, or cut it.

> *Before:* The exchange halted trading for an hour, underscoring the fragility of modern markets.
> *After:* The exchange halted trading for an hour. Three regional exchanges that route orders through it halted with it.

**BP-13. Unearned aphorism.** A portable formula that sounds profound anywhere ("X is the currency of Y," "X is not a tool but a mirror") is profound nowhere; an aphorism must be built from the piece's own material or not appear at all.

> *Before:* Trust is the currency of maritime trade.
> *After:* A tanker that turns off its transponder loses its insurance before it loses its cargo.

### Rhythm

**BP-14. Uniform sentence length.** A paragraph of sentences all the same length reads at one flat pitch regardless of content; give the load-bearing point a short sentence and the qualification a long one.

> *Before:* The fleet left port on Monday morning. The escort joined them near the strait. The convoy reached the terminal safely. The insurers noted the outcome with interest.
> *After:* The fleet left port on Monday morning and picked up its escort near the strait, where the previous three convoys had been challenged. This one was not.

**BP-15. Dash and comma clustering.** Dashes and comma-spliced asides are legitimate instruments, but three in a paragraph chop it into fragments; when they cluster, promote one aside to its own sentence and fold the rest in.

> *Before:* The port — the largest on the coast — handles grain, oil — mostly refined — and, increasingly, transshipped containers — a recent development.
> *After:* The port, the largest on the coast, handles grain, refined oil and — a recent development — transshipped containers.

**BP-16. Staccato runs.** One short sentence lands a point; four in a row manufacture drama the material has not earned. Keep the strongest and give the others verbs and clauses.

> *Before:* The cable was cut. Nobody claimed it. Nobody saw it. The sea keeps secrets.
> *After:* The cable was cut, and nobody claimed the act or saw it done.

## Part II — Voice (BPV-01 to BPV-08)

The voice of Roderick Jones, in two registers sharing one toolkit. Register A, the historian's essay: longer periodic sentences, archival specificity, argument through narrative sequence, conclusions held back until the evidence is laid. Register B, the futurist security column: present tense, forward-leaning, compressed historical evidence in service of trajectory claims, shorter paragraphs, harder topic sentences. Each rule below is testable; each carries a pass and a fail.

**BPV-01. The coined concept.** Each major piece coins or carries one to three named concepts and makes the strongest the anchor of the close. Coinages are protected absolutely: never paraphrased, never varied, repeated verbatim every time.

> *Pass:* "Actuarial peace" appears four times, verbatim each time, and the final paragraph turns on it.
> *Fail:* "Buffer markets" is coined in section two and returns in section four as "these intermediary exchanges."

**BPV-02. The analytical spine.** A piece moves incident, then system, then trajectory, then response. Test the structure, not only the sentences: a draft that opens with the system and never grounds it in an incident has drifted, and the fix is structural.

> *Pass:* A cut cable (incident), the repair-ship market that services it (system), where that market is heading (trajectory), what a navy should do about it (response).
> *Fail:* Three sections on the repair-ship market with the cut cable mentioned once, in passing, at the end.

**BPV-03. Historical analogy with a limiting case.** Analogy is the primary explanatory move, and an analogy is only trustworthy once the writer shows where it breaks. Flag any analogy offered without a negative or control case.

> *Pass:* "Data centres are this century's coaling stations, with one difference that matters: coal ran out, and a chokepoint on compute does not."
> *Fail:* "Data centres are this century's coaling stations." (Full stop, no test.)

**BPV-04. Calibrated hedging.** A hedge is a placed instrument, one per claim, positioned where the uncertainty actually lives. Protect placed hedges; cut hedges that merely pad ("could potentially possibly").

> *Pass:* "The pattern will probably hold through the decade; the evidence past that is thin."
> *Fail:* "It could perhaps be argued that the pattern might possibly hold."

**BPV-05. The aphoristic riff.** A section of weight closes on one aphorism, usually a paired construction, built from the piece's own material. One per section; never a portable formula.

> *Pass:* "The nineteenth century built buffer states; the twenty-first builds buffer markets." (In a piece about both.)
> *Fail:* Two aphorisms in one section, or "history doesn't repeat, but it rhymes" in any section.

**BPV-06. The forward-leaning ending.** A piece ends on the open problem, never on a summary and never on a generic upbeat close. If the last paragraph could be deleted without losing a claim, it is a summary; replace it with the question the piece has earned.

> *Pass:* "The convoys work. What has no answer yet is who commands them when the coalition's mandate lapses in March."
> *Fail:* "In sum, maritime security is entering a new era, and the stakes could not be higher."

**BPV-07. British English.** British spelling and usage by default (globalised, defence, programme, theatre); American spellings survive only inside quotations and proper nouns.

> *Pass:* "The programme globalised quickly; the U.S. Department of Defense objected."
> *Fail:* "The program globalized quickly."

**BPV-08. The through-line.** The recurring question beneath the work: who exercises real authority when formal structures cannot supply it. Not enforced per piece; but when a draft gestures at this question and fails to land it, say so, because a gesture at the through-line is a promise to the reader.

> *Pass:* A piece on port cyberattacks that ends by asking who governs the scheduling software no state controls.
> *Fail:* A piece that notes "this raises questions of governance" and moves on.

## Part III — Precedence

Where Part II modifies Part I, the modification is stated here and nowhere else. Voice wins.

**P-1. The coinage shield.** BPV-01 exempts coined concepts from BP-04 (synonym cycling's licence to cut workless echoes) and from any economy cut under BP-05: a coinage is repeated verbatim however often the argument needs it, and no variation is ever introduced for elegance.

**P-2. The hedging distinction.** BPV-04 modifies BP-02 and BP-03: a placed hedge is not a weasel word. "Probably," positioned once where the uncertainty lives, is calibration; the same word scattered three times in a paragraph is padding, and the padding falls under BP-02 as before.

**P-3. The aphorism licence.** BPV-05 modifies BP-13: one aphorism per major section is permitted, provided it is built from the piece's own material. BP-13 continues to govern portable formulas and any second aphorism in the same section.

**P-4. The dash position.** Dashes are standard equipment in both registers, carrying the periodic asides of Register A and the compressed interjections of Register B (see the pass examples under BPV-05 and BPV-06). No rule in this document bans them; they are reviewed under BP-15 only, and a cluster of dashes is a rhythm fault, not a punctuation offence.

## Sources

Part I adapts, in fresh words and with fresh examples, quality patterns first catalogued in [blader/humanizer](https://github.com/blader/humanizer) (MIT License, Copyright © 2025 Siqi Chen), itself built on Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" guide: inflated significance, promotional register, superficial participial tack-ons, vague attribution, filler, generic conclusions, weasel hedging, synonym cycling and outline-shaped prose. Humanizer's pure detection heuristics (bans on dashes, curly quotes, title case and the like) were deliberately not adopted: BetterProse edits for the reader, not the detector. The plain-style rules restate the Orwell/Gowers tradition: prefer the concrete to the abstract, the active to the passive where the actor matters, the short word where meaning is equal; cut what does no work; never let a metaphor do the arguing.
