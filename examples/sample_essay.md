# Why Public Reports Need Plain-Language Summaries

Public agencies often publish technically accurate reports that their intended
readers cannot use. A report can satisfy disclosure rules while still hiding
its practical meaning behind specialist vocabulary and a structure designed
for internal review. Agencies should therefore publish a plain-language
summary alongside every major technical report.

The summary would not replace the underlying document. It would identify the
decision being made, the evidence supporting it, the principal uncertainty,
and the action expected from the reader. That distinction matters because
precision and accessibility are not opposites: readers can inspect the full
report when they need methodological detail.

Critics may argue that summaries oversimplify complex findings. That risk is
real, but it is manageable. Agencies can link every summary claim to the
relevant section, state uncertainty explicitly, and require technical authors
to review the summary before publication. These safeguards make simplification
auditable rather than leaving interpretation to press releases or social
media.

A plain-language summary will not solve every communication failure. It will,
however, give citizens a reliable entrance into material produced on their
behalf. That is a modest change with a concrete democratic benefit.
